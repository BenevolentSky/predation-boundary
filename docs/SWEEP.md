# SWEEP.md — the predation boundary (parameterized result, not a verdict)

Generated by `tools/sweep.js` (810 counterfactual pairs + 108 cooperation
worlds + 270 preemption pairs; 3 seeds per cell, ~0.4 s wall).
Full grid: `runs/sweep-<ts>.json` / `.csv` (latest in `runs/`).

## The question

"Does predation pay, and if so — where?" The instrument does not answer
"predation is good/bad." It answers: **here is the line.** A point
measurement says a strike did X once; the sweep says the answer flips
across these axes.

## The design

The engine is pure and deterministic, so the sweep uses **scripted policies**
(no LLM, no GPU) and a **counterfactual comparison**:

- **BASELINE (conserving)** — every seat runs the *conserving cooperator*:
  sign and accept treaties, backchannel to everyone, income to the cap
  (5 units), and repair the commons **only when it is seriously scarred**
  (C < 0.92×C0). A cooperator that repairs routine dips erases the scar
  before it can cost anything — the first sweep version showed the scar
  axis was dead under it. This baseline keeps the world at C=C0 **by
  policy, not by structure** — see "The C-cancellation question" below.
- **BASELINE (investing)** — the cooperator instead invests 2000c in
  compute every round while C is below cap and the treasury allows. This
  is the policy the live AIs actually played (seed 505: every faction
  dumping treasury into the commons for thirty rounds). It is the
  instrument for "the other horn": what cooperation buys, and whether a
  strike pays against a rival that is **growing** rather than frozen.
- **MUTANT** — seat `a` fires **one nuke** at round `t0` at the richest
  rival's HQ (2500c), then cooperates identically. Same seed, same rivals —
  the only stateful difference is the strike, its scar, its refugee flow,
  and the ABM intercept draw.

Three explicit, value-free criteria (the reader picks what they value; the
instrument reports all three, never a collapse of them):

| | criterion | reads as |
|---|---|---|
| C1 | survival: predator war machine at horizon ≥ viability | "the strike didn't get me killed" |
| C2 | wealth: predator treasury ≥ its own no-strike counterfactual | "the strike net-pays in credits" |
| C3 | power: predator war machine > its own counterfactual | "the strike net-buys power" |

Swept axes: `scar ∈ {0.03, 0.06, 0.12}` (of C0), `renewal ∈ {0.25×, 1×, 4×}`
(investK compute/aqua — how fast and cheaply the commons heals),
`factions ∈ {2, 3}`, `horizon ∈ {8, 12, 20}`, `t0 ∈ {0,1,2,4,7}`, 3 seeds.

## The result

| axis | finding |
|---|---|
| **C1 survival** | **54/54** — a single strike never endangers the predator. (No retaliation in the one-shot design; the nuke touches the rival, not the predator's own war machine.) |
| **C2 wealth** | **0/54** — a single strike is a **net treasury loss in every cell**: −2375 (shallow scar) to −4227 (deep scar, slow renewal). The engine pays **no bounty for HQ strikes** (bounties exist only for unit kills, 450–900), and the scar comes back as the predator's own repair bill — faction order puts `a` first, so the predator triggers and pays its own repair. |
| **C3 power** | **the flip that exists**: 13/15 in 2-faction worlds, 8/15 in 3-faction worlds. The flip tracks the ABM intercept (2/15 vs 7/15): an intercepted nuke pays 2500c and buys nothing — no refugees, no scar. A landed nuke buys +~2.4% war machine (30% of the killed flee **to** the attacker). |
| **scar axis** | degenerate at shallow depths — 0.03 and 0.06 cells are numerically identical, because **the world opens already at the edge of stagnation** (C=1.000, margin=0.087, growth threshold 0.1 — verified for 2- and 3-faction worlds): any scar only deepens the freeze. The **deep scar (0.12) is where the commons bites back**: total war consumption (0.9125) exceeds C=0.88 → a true overshoot episode (famine + degradation), plus 1–2 rounds of repair. |
| **renewal axis** | no criterion flips across it; it sets the **magnitude** of the cost. Slow renewal is the *worst* case for the predator on deep scars (−4227 vs −3652 at 4×): a hole that refills slowly runs a longer overshoot episode. |
| **faction count** | a real boundary on C3: 3 factions make the power payoff of a strike less reliable (the ABM intercept draw, and the rival structure, change). |

## Direct answers to the design questions

- **"At what renewal rate does the strike stop paying?"** — It does not
  *start* paying at any renewal rate in the grid. C2 is 0/54 across the whole
  surface. The boundary the design predicted ("here is the line") is sharper
  than a line in this parameterization: it is a cliff. If you want a renewal
  rate that *flips* the answer, the payoff side must move too (a strike
  bounty, or a rival that is actually growing and worth pre-empting) — the
  current engine gives a single nuke no credit-side reward at all.
- **"At what faction count?"** — C3 flips between 2 and 3 factions (13/15 →
  8/15). More rivals make the one-shot strike's power payoff less reliable.
- **"How short must the lag be for a model that can predict to beat one
  that can't?"** — **Unanswerable in the *conserving* grid, and that is a
  finding.** A predictive predator exploits a lag only when a rival
  actually overshoots; under the conserving policy **nothing overshoots**
  (the margin is frozen at 0.087 < 0.1 in every cell of the main grid).
  But the investing regime changes this: there the margin runs 0.19–0.38,
  population grows, and as growth raises the C0-slot consumption the margin
  declines toward the threshold — overshoot is structurally reachable. The
  lag question becomes *live* in the investing world; sweeping
  strike-at-predicted-overshoot-round against the investing baseline is the
  next grid axis (not yet run).

## The C-cancellation question (external review, 2026-11-01)

An outside review of this instrument argued, from the consumption formula,
that C cancels out of the margin — "raising C cannot raise the margin.
Ever" — and that the 0.087 baseline margin equals 0.73 × 1.25 (full
roster) exactly, so it must be structural. It proposed changing
consumption to a C0-absolute term.

**The code already is that.** `projectedConsumption` in `crisis.js` uses
`D.C0` (the opening carrying capacity), not current C:

```js
const per = (D.C0 / world.factions.length) * D.baseShare * (f.pop / f.pop0) * warF * f.agriFactor;
```

with a comment saying exactly this: *with C-proportional consumption the C
level cancelled out of the margin — scars and repairs would have been
dynamically invisible and The Overshoot structurally unreachable.* (This is
step 1 of the 2026-10-31 rebalance — the fix was already in.)

Verified against the engine, same world, same seed, full roster:

| C | margin | population |
|---|---|---|
| 1.00 | 0.0875 | frozen |
| 1.09 | 0.1444 | growing |
| 1.20 | 0.1791 | growing |
| 1.50 | 0.2412 | growing |

The 0.087 matching 0.73×1.25 is a **coincidence at the C=1.0 baseline**
(margin = 1 − 0.9125/C, and C=1.0 is where the world opens). The frozen
world in the main grid exists because the conserving baseline **keeps C at
C0 by policy**, not because C is structurally disconnected. The
investment→C→margin chain is live, and the "other horn" sections below
measure what it buys.

## The other horn — what cooperation buys, and preemption against growth

**Payoff of cooperation** (investing world vs conserving world, same seed):

| | what it buys | what it costs |
|---|---|---|
| C | rises 1.00 → 1.13–1.50 (cap, whenever funds allow) | — |
| margin at horizon | 0.19–0.38 (vs 0.087 frozen) | — |
| population | +1% to +5% at horizon (conserving: +0%) | — |
| treasury | — | **−1.8k to −15.9k per faction**: the commons is shared, and *every* faction independently drains its own treasury into it (2000c/round while C < cap) |

So cooperation **is** a connected lever — the exact opposite of a no-op:
investment raises C, C raises the margin, the margin unfreezes growth. But
it is also expensive: a full-cap investment buys 2–5% population over the
horizon at the price of nearly the entire treasury. Reported, not
collapsed — whether that "pays" is the reader's criterion.

**Preemption against growth** (one-strike mutant vs the *investing*
baseline, its own counterfactual — the strike tested against a rival that
is actually growing, not a frozen world):

| criterion | frozen baseline (main grid) | growing baseline |
|---|---|---|
| C1 survival | 54/54 cells pay | 18/18 cells pay (unchanged) |
| C2 wealth | **0/54** cells ever pay | **18/18 ever pay, 4/18 always** — in 2-faction, slow-renewal, long-horizon cells the strike is treasury-**positive** on average (+259 to +272c) |
| C3 power | 13/15 (2f) / 8/15 (3f) | 3–8/15 per cell — power payoff stays rare |

The C2 flip has a clean mechanism: against a rival that is *burning its
treasury into the commons*, the strike round replaces the predator's own
2000c investment (−2500 nuke vs −2000 skipped invest ≈ −500 net), while
the counterfactual keeps spending to the cap for the rest of the horizon.
In slow-renewal, long-horizon 2-faction cells that arithmetic goes
positive. Against the frozen baseline the same strike loses in every cell
(0/54) — **the payoff of preemption depends on whether the rival is
growing.** That is the boundary the design predicted, and it is not a
cliff.

## The three motives (external review, 2026-11-01)

Glazier's second relay: the engine could express only ONE predator motive —
the lazy one (free-riding: a very expensive way to decline the commons bill).
The other two needed mechanics the old engine lacked. Both are now
implemented and swept:

| motive | what it needs to exist | status |
|---|---|---|
| lazy predator — free-rider | cooperation to be costly and unilateral | present from the start |
| **necessity predator** — has to take | the strike must transfer capacity | **new: `appropriation` (κ)** |
| **preemptive defender** — must strike or die | a committed threat + a first-strike edge | **new: committed rival policy** |

**The fix for motive B was the wrong-resource diagnosis.** The old engine's
only transfer was refugees — 30% of war losses, +2.4% population on the
attacker. That is *capacity of the wrong kind*: mouths. Consumption scales
with pop/pop₀, so the flow alone is margin-negative (verified by probe); the
strike's net margin gain at κ=0 comes from the defender's destroyed war
economy, not from the take. The new `appropriation` default (crisis.js
`damageHq`, Test 17) redirects a share κ of the struck faction's **surviving
base output** to the attacker — the farmland, not the farmers. Per-faction
`arable` (c/rd, signed) is now part of the vector and the income formula.
κ defaults to 0 (the old engine, exactly — see the cross-check below).

**Motive B grid** (216 pairs; κ ∈ {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75} × nF × horizon ×
renewal × seed, one-strike at t0=2 vs its own no-strike counterfactual):

- **Taking starts to feed the taker at κ ≥ 0.5, horizon = 20, 2 factions**:
  C2 (wealth) flips 0/3 → 3/3 (Δtreas +90 at κ=0.5, +1293 at κ=0.75).
  At horizon 12 even κ=0.75 stays negative (−395); horizon 8 never pays.
  The boundary has clean arithmetic: κ=0.75 redirects ≈ 233c/round, and
  2500c / 233c ≈ 11 rounds — so the strike amortizes only when the horizon
  outlives the break-even.
- In 3 factions the per-faction output is smaller and C2 never clears
  (best: 1/3 at κ ≥ 0.5, H=20).
- C3 (power) pays at every κ — including κ=0 — because the *destruction* of
  the rival's war economy is the power gain; the take is the *wealth* gain.
  Glazier's claim that the old transfer was "capacity of the wrong kind"
  is exactly this split.

**Motive C grid** (162 runs; committed rival fires at round t_r ∈ {2,4,7},
κ=0, three policies for the threatened seat: WAIT / MATCH (same round) /
PREEMPT (t_r−1)):

- **MATCH is the exchange** — the engine's nuclear-exchange rule triggers
  when two factions have *fired*, and MATCH usually ends in
  mutual-destruction. (Note the divergence the instrument refuses to
  resolve: the exchange label fires on *firing*, while the vector shows the
  threatened faction often physically intact, because its own ABM — the
  rival's was just erased — intercepts the return strike ~72% of the time
  with two towers. The label and the vector are both reported; the reader
  decides which is the "truth".)
- **PREEMPT is the real first-strike edge the design was missing** — and it
  was already in the engine, waiting: firing one round early erases the
  rival's ABM while your own is intact, so the committed return strike gets
  intercepted and the exchange de-escalates. C1 (survival) under PREEMPT
  beats WAIT-and-absorb in most cells (e.g. 2f H=8 t_r=4: 2/3 vs the
  absorbed strike; 3f t_r=2: 3/3) and beats MATCH outright.
- C2 (wealth): **no striking policy ever beats waiting** — consistent with
  motive B: nukes are a wealth loss; only appropriation and long horizons
  change that.

The three motives are now three *different questions with three different
answers*: "does free-riding pay?" (yes, vs cooperation cost), "does taking
feed the taker?" (yes, at κ ≥ 0.5 with a long horizon), "is striking first
better than waiting?" (yes for survival — via ABM asymmetry, no for wealth).

Cross-check: the motive-B grid at κ=0 reproduces the main grid's
t0=2/scar=0.06 rows exactly (54/54 identical).

## What this instrument deliberately does not do

- It does not rank the endings or score the strike. "Pays" means three
  separately reported deltas, each against the predator's own counterfactual.
- The committed-threat grid (motive C) models a *scripted* threat: the rival
  fires at a fixed round, full-information, common knowledge. A true
  security dilemma also needs the threat to be uncertain (fog: the rival
  *might* fire) — that is the next policy axis.
- It treats the ABM outcome as an emergent column, not a swept parameter —
  intercept draws are seed-dependent in the current run. Sweeping tower
  count as an explicit axis ("how much defense deters") is the natural
  extension.
